Writing

A note on craft.

By Sasha Rivers · 14 May 2026 · 6 min

Software has a habit of asking for attention it has not earned. It blinks, pulses, slides in from the corner, suggests, nudges, congratulates. The result is a class of tools that appear busy and feel exhausting — a quality the people who make them rarely intend, but rarely resist either.

We started Atelier because we wanted to spend our days inside something quieter. The question we ask of every surface is the same the printer asks of a page: is anything here doing work that the page itself is not already doing? If a button can be a sentence, let it be a sentence. Take away what you can, then take away a little more.

We choose calm over loud, depth over surface, and clarity over cleverness.

A page, not a panel.

The instinct to wrap everything in a card is a recent one. For most of typographic history a page got its structure from rules, columns, and white space. The card era — borders that round, shadows that bloom, surfaces that float — has the unfortunate property of making every kind of content look like the same kind of content: a tile, awaiting a click.

We prefer the older approach. Set a measure of about thirty-eight ems and trust the line to carry the eye. When something genuinely deserves to be set apart, a single hairline does more than a panel ever could.

Friction, on purpose.

We add friction where it's earned. Before publishing, before deleting, before sending — there's a brief surface that tells you, in plain sentences, what's about to happen. Not a yes/no modal. A summary, written like a colleague would write it. The user is doing work. The product should treat that work as something that deserves a last look.
