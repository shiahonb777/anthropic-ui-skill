A note on craft.

Atelier · May MMXXVI · § I — On the page

Software has a habit of asking for attention it has not earned. It blinks, pulses, slides in from the corner, suggests, nudges, congratulates. The result is a class of tools that appear busy and feel exhausting — a quality the people who make them rarely intend, but rarely resist either.

We started Atelier because we wanted to spend our days inside something quieter. The question we ask of every surface is the same the printer asks of a page: is anything here doing work that the page itself is not already doing? If a button can be a sentence, let it be a sentence. Take away what you can, then take away a little more.

§ III — Essay

Restraint is a feature, not a feature list.

By Sasha Rivers · 6 min · Manifesto

Every product we ship begins with a list of things it will not do. Not for fashion, but because each refusal frees the remaining surface to do its one job well. The customer notices the absence of clutter the way you notice a quiet room: not all at once, but as the steady, continuing relief of not being addressed by everything.

I.

A page, not a panel.

The instinct to wrap everything in a card is a recent one. For most of typographic history a page got its structure from rules, columns, and white space. The card era — borders that round, shadows that bloom, surfaces that float — has the unfortunate property of making every kind of content look like the same kind of content: a tile, awaiting a click.1

Take away what you can, then take away a little more. From the manifesto, § I
II.

Reading, not scanning.

A serif body is the most distinctive choice a software product can make in 2026. It says, without saying so, that what's here is meant to be read — not glanced at on the way to the next CTA. We pair it with a geometric sans for the spine of the interface (navigation, labels, chrome) and reserve italics for emphasis the way a writer would, sparingly and on purpose.

Numbers, too, get attention they rarely receive. In running prose we use old-style numerals — 1234567890 — which sit on the baseline like lowercase letters. In tables, captions, and chrome we switch to lining, tabular numerals — 1234567890 — so columns of figures align without help.

III.

Friction, on purpose.

We add friction where it's earned. Before publishing, before deleting, before sending — there's a brief surface that tells you, in plain sentences, what's about to happen. Not a yes/no modal. A summary, written like a colleague would write it.2 The user is doing work. The product should treat that work as something that deserves a last look.

  1. For more on this, see Bringhurst's The Elements of Typographic Style, particularly the chapter on shaping the page. We borrow more from him than from any modern UI book. ↩
  2. See also: Anthropic's Plan Mode, where the model summarizes its intended action and waits for the user before proceeding. The pause is a feature, not an accident. ↩

§ VI — Studio

A studio of eleven, hiring slowly.

We're a small team in Portland and Brooklyn — designers, engineers, and a researcher who spends most of her days reading. We work in two-week cycles, ship when it's right, and answer every customer email by hand.

We have no growth team and no plans to start one. We're more interested in keeping the work good than in keeping the calendar full.

§ VII — Answers

Plain answers to the questions we hear most.

  1. Is there a free plan? No. We've tried it. The economics push us toward designs we don't believe in. There's a 30-day trial instead, and we'll give a longer one if you ask.
  2. Do you train models on customer documents? No. We don't, and we won't. Your work is your work.
  3. Will Atelier integrate with my existing tools? Some. We'd rather support a few well than many poorly. The current list is on the integrations page.
  4. Can I export everything? Yes. Plain Markdown, with sources and citations preserved. We send you a zip and we don't keep a copy.
  5. Why is the page background not white? Because pure white reads as harsh and corporate. The slight cream is one of the small choices that, taken together, makes the work feel like a printed page rather than a panel.

Two weeks, no card required. We answer every reply by hand, usually within a day.
